# The customer: published facts about Fogline's world

Research date: 2026-10-24. Every number below came from a page or file I opened. Numbers in square brackets point to
the Sources list at the end. Where a figure comes from a company that sells credit insurance or software, the row says
"vendor survey". Fogline Supply Co. is made up (`company/profile.md`: 26 employees, about $8.1M revenue, 38 customers on
trade credit). The numbers below describe real US businesses like it, not Fogline.

Two cautions that apply to the whole file.

- Most of these are surveys of people who chose to answer. None is a count of every US business. The scope is given in
  each row.
- Nothing here is legal advice. Section 5 quotes the rules the decline letter is modelled on. A lawyer should read them
  before the desk sends a real letter.

## 3. How small wholesalers run credit today

### Sourced

- **What the application asks for.** NACM's guide to credit applications (updated 2019) says the references section
  asks for "Trade suppliers (usually three)" with a contact for each, and "bank(s), lenders, credit card companies"
  with account numbers and "authorization for release of information". It warns "the applicant will always attempt to
  provide the best available references, and they are not necessarily representative of payment history", and that
  "Information received on the application should be regarded as unverified fact" [12].
- **Personal guarantee and the guarantor's credit report.** The same guide says each guarantor needs a separate
  signature and "must authorize the creditor to perform a thorough credit investigation, including the review of the
  personal guarantor's personal credit report" [12]. NACM Commercial Services says a consumer report "can be helpful
  when accessing a small business that may not have much on their commercial credit report" [20].
- **Reference checking is often handed off.** NACM's processing service contacts "up to four (4) business references"
  and merges the replies into the NACM National Trade Credit Report. Non-member tiers are Rush (final report the next
  business day), One-Week (5 business days) and Two-Week (10 business days) [13].
- **How far it is digital.** In the 2025 NACM and Nuvo survey, 32% of respondents had fully digitized their credit
  evaluation and 44% were partway [11]. The survey also says "most now request two or more references rather than
  three or more" [11]. An NACM Business Credit Management article says 46% of credit departments process new
  applications by email and 24% on paper. It does not name the survey, sample or date [14].
- **How many people touch a decision.** In Creditsafe's 2023 mini study of 334 US financial managers, 63% said up to
  five people are involved in a credit decision, 22% said six to 10, and 14% said more than 10 [15]. Creditsafe sells
  credit reports.
- **The delays.** In the NACM and Nuvo survey, the greatest delay in approving net terms was reworking applications with
  customers (39%), then bank reference response time (34%), searching and analyzing credit bureau reports (34%) and
  trade reference response time (32%) [11].

### Not found

I found no published figure for who does the credit work at a small distributor (owner, bookkeeper or one credit
person), or for how many small wholesalers use spreadsheets or accounting software for it. NAW and CRF searches
returned nothing usable. A QuickBooks bookkeeping figure appeared in a search summary, but I could not open its page,
so I did not use it.

### General description (not sourced)

This is how I would describe the customer. It is not a finding, and it should not be quoted as one.

- At a company the size of Fogline, credit decisions usually sit with a controller, bookkeeper or owner, often next to
  other duties. Sales brings the customer in and pushes for a yes.
- The checks are a paper or PDF application, a phone call to two or three references, a business credit report and,
  when there is a personal guarantee, a personal credit report on the owner.
- The records live in the accounting package and a spreadsheet of credit limits. Limits are reviewed when something
  goes wrong.
- If you can get one 20-minute interview with a real bookkeeper or controller at a small distributor, a quote from it
  is worth more than any row above.

Which to use.

- **For minutes per application: none credible.** Nothing above times the work. The Creditsafe bar "up to a full day
  (8 hours)" is a ceiling for 75% of respondents, not an average, and it mixes work with waiting. The HighRadius dollar
  figure cannot be turned into minutes without a wage, and it has no source.
- **For elapsed time: the NACM and Nuvo 2025 distribution.** It is recent and it names causes. Those causes are mostly
  waiting for other people (customers, banks, trade references), which is also what a desk that works in parallel can
  cut. Cross-check: APQC's median of 5.0 calendar days falls inside the same spread, between the 3 days that 46% beat
  and the week or more that 29% need. The three sources measure different things, so none confirms another exactly.
- **How to word it in the pitch.** "Surveys of credit professionals put approval of a new business customer at under
  three days for 46%, and a week or more for more than a quarter [11]." If a minutes figure is needed, time the desk's
  own run, label it as measured on the demo case, and do not compare it with a published minutes number, because none
  exists.

## 5. Adverse action notices

The desk's rule DCL-1 (`rules/credit-rules.yaml`) models its decline letter on two laws. The Equal Credit Opportunity
Act, written out as Regulation B, covers all credit, including credit one business gives another. The Fair Credit
Reporting Act, section 615(a), applies when a consumer report was used.

### Regulation B, 12 CFR 1002.9 [22][23][24]

It is easy to read the business-credit rule as a split by revenue ($1 million or less, and over $1 million). For trade
credit the split does not apply. A wholesaler selling on Net 30 gives trade credit, and trade credit goes to the
second group whatever the buyer's size. The text:

- (a)(3)(i) is for "a business that had gross revenues of $1 million or less in its preceding fiscal year (other than
  an extension of trade credit, credit incident to a factoring agreement, or other similar types of business
  credit)". Those applicants get the consumer rules in (a)(1) and (2), including the 30-day notice, with three
  changes. The notice of action "may be given orally or in writing", the right to reasons "may be given at the time of
  application", and a phone application can be answered by phone.
- (a)(3)(ii) is for "a business that had gross revenues in excess of $1 million in its preceding fiscal year or an
  extension of trade credit, credit incident to a factoring agreement, or other similar types of business credit".
  The creditor must:
  - "(A) Notify the applicant, within a reasonable time, orally or in writing, of the action taken; and"
  - "(B) Provide a written statement of the reasons for adverse action and the ECOA notice specified in paragraph (b)(1)
    of this section if the applicant makes a written request for the reasons within 60 days of the creditor's
    notification."
- The official interpretation of Regulation B says trade credit "generally is limited to a financing arrangement that
  involves a buyer and a seller" (comment 9(a)(3)-2). The comment's own example is "a supplier who finances the sale
  of equipment, supplies, or inventory" [28].

For Fogline this means (a)(3)(ii) applies to every trade-credit decline, whatever the applicant's size.

Timing.

- "Reasonable time" has a safe harbor. Comment 9(a)(3)-5 says "Notice provided in accordance with the timing
  requirements of § 1002.9(a)(1) is deemed reasonable in all instances" [28]. Section (a)(1)(i) is "30 days after
  receiving a completed application". For an incomplete application, (c)(1) also uses "30 days" [22].
- The 60-day window is for the applicant's written request for reasons. NACM's sample declination clause matches
  this: "within 60 days from the date the Applicant is notified", with the seller sending reasons "within 30 days of
  receiving the Applicant's request" [12]. The CFPB's sample Form C-8 says the same [25].
- Comment 9(a)(3)-4 lets a business creditor "follow the rules governing consumer credit", so a letter that gives the
  written reasons up front, as DCL-1 does, is allowed [28].

What the reasons must look like (1002.9(b)(2)) [22][28].

- "The statement of reasons ... must be specific and indicate the principal reason(s) for the adverse action."
- "Statements that the adverse action was based on the creditor's internal standards or policies or that the
  applicant ... failed to achieve a qualifying score on the creditor's credit scoring system are insufficient."
- Comment 9(b)(2)-2: reasons "must relate to and accurately describe the factors actually considered or scored by a
  creditor."
- Comment 9(b)(2)-3: the creditor "need not describe how or why a factor adversely affected an applicant".
- Comment 9(b)(2)-1: "disclosure of more than four reasons is not likely to be helpful".
- Comment 9(b)(2)-8: when an automatic-denial factor drives the decision, "the creditor must disclose that specific
  factor."
- Comment 9(b)(2)-9: telling the applicant that a credit report was used "does not satisfy the ECOA requirement to
  disclose specific reasons."
- The ECOA notice text is in 1002.9(b)(1) ("The Federal Equal Credit Opportunity Act prohibits creditors from
  discriminating against credit applicants on the basis of race, color, religion, ...") and must be "substantially
  similar" [22]. Form C-7 is the CFPB's sample business decline letter. Its example reasons are "Value or type of
  collateral not sufficient; Lack of established earnings record; Slow or past due in trade or loan payments" [25].

### FCRA section 615(a), 15 U.S.C. 1681m(a) [26]

Trigger: "If any person takes any adverse action with respect to any consumer that is based in whole or in part on any
information contained in a consumer report". The person must then give:

- "the name, address, and telephone number of the consumer reporting agency ... that furnished the report";
- "a statement that the consumer reporting agency did not make the decision to take the adverse action and is unable to
  provide the consumer the specific reasons why the adverse action was taken";
- notice of the consumer's right "to obtain ... a free copy of a consumer report on the consumer from the consumer
  reporting agency", with "an indication of the 60-day period"; and
- notice of the right "to dispute ... with a consumer reporting agency the accuracy or completeness of any information
  in a consumer report furnished by the agency".

The Reg B sample forms carry the same words in plain language, for example "The reporting agency played no part in our
decision and is unable to supply specific reasons why we have denied credit to you" [25].

Does 615(a) reach a guarantor? The FTC staff report on the FCRA (July 2011) says it does not, as the staff read the
law. Under "Primary credit applicant, co-applicant, and guarantor" it says "only an 'applicant' experiences 'adverse
action' in the context of a credit transaction ... A co-applicant is an 'applicant' but a guarantor is not." Elsewhere
it says "a creditor need not provide a guarantor with an adverse action notice, even if the application is denied in
whole or in part based upon information from the consumer report of the guarantor" [27]. A sole proprietor who applies
is an applicant, so the notice is owed there. The same report says a creditor has a permissible purpose to pull a
consumer report in a business credit deal only for an individual who "is or will be personally liable" as co-signer or
guarantor [27]. Both statements are from 2011 FTC staff views, and I did not check for later changes. Sending the
notice to a guarantor anyway, as DCL-1 does, goes beyond that reading.

### What this means for DCL-1 and the "unable to verify" reason

DCL-1 says fraud cases use the reason "unable to verify information". No source I opened says that wording is
enough.

- The CFPB's sample reasons (Form C-1, Appendix C) include "Unable to verify credit references", "Unable to verify
  employment", "Unable to verify income" and "Unable to verify residence". Each names what could not be verified.
  There is no generic "unable to verify information" line and no identity or fraud line, though "Other" is allowed [25].
- Comment 9(b)(2)-2 requires the reason to "accurately describe the factors actually considered" [28]. If the
  deciding factor was a failed identity check, a reason that hides it is a risk. If the deciding factor was a fraud
  score, comment 9(b)(2)-8 is the closest rule [28].
- Nothing I read says a creditor may keep the name of a fraud check secret. Nothing says it must name the vendor or
  the test. The rule is about the factor, not the tool. Treat "may use a generic fraud reason" as unconfirmed, and
  ask a lawyer before a real letter goes out.
- Safer wording that stays inside what the sources show: name the factor in plain words ("we could not verify the
  identity of the guarantor"), skip the check's name and score, and keep the bureau paragraph for any decline that
  used a consumer report.
