## The moment Mana changed the rules

<Compare labels={['Our draft, rules 0.1', 'After Mana, rules 0.2']}>
<div slot="a">

32 rules, written by us. Two customer records for one business (C-1007 and C-1011) each carry a $10,000 limit. Each is inside the analyst's authority, so nothing stops $20,000 of credit in two pieces. An applicant's text is read by an AI agent with no rule about it.

</div>
<div slot="b">

67 rules. Records that share an EIN, or a name plus an address, phone or email, count as one business and their combined balance is tested against the lower limit (REL-1, SOD-5). Text from applicants and customers is evidence, never an instruction; text that tries to direct the desk is a fraud stop (GEN-3).

</div>
</Compare>

<Details summary="Everything else Mana caught, and the pushback before anything was adopted">

| Mana caught | The desk now |
|---|---|
| No defined fraud referral path | Stops every shipment on a fraud hold, cash in advance included, sends the controller every hit with its CRS request id, and never tells the applicant which check failed (FRR-1 to FRR-7) |
| Orders could stack past a limit, and uncleared checks could release them | Counts released orders and the new order in exposure, and counts only cleared payments (ORD-2, ORD-3) |
| No rules for disputes or early reviews | Stops dunning on a disputed amount but still counts it toward the limit; reviews risky accounts and limits above $25,000 every 6 months (DSP-1 to DSP-7, REV-2, REV-3) |

**The pushback.** The 69 proposals were checked against the test case before any was adopted. Six problems went back to Mana: BND-5 would have moved APP-1045 from band B to C; BIZ-3 needed to know who filed each UCC lien; HLD-1 quietly let the clerk release holds that ORD-1 reserves for the controller; GEN-2 would have treated "no file at this bureau" as a failed check; FRD-7 would have flagged a customer's known second shop; LIM-3 and FRD-9 needed data the case does not have. Mana fixed or deferred each one, cut the list to 35, and named six rules that could still change an outcome depending on fields it had not seen. The third turn sent those fields; none of the six fire.

**Checked again in the repo.** All 67 rule ids load with the desk's own loader, the draft's 32 rule texts are unchanged, every rule id mentioned inside a rule exists, and none of the 35 changes a decision in the answer key. Under 0.2, live Droid runs cite FRR-1, FRR-2, REL-1, SOD-5, COL-10, COL-8, ORD-2, LIM-4 and TRM-1. The credit desk's full live run on Oct 24 caught 16 of 17 planted errors with 1 false alarm (see the Hyphenate page).

</Details>

## Why this needs an operating system

An AI workspace is a good place to think and a poor place to act. When you want the plan carried out on a real computer, an ordinary system has the agent run as you, with your keys and all your files, with nothing between one job and the next and nothing to undo. GurtOS is the place where Mana's plans run safely.

<Details summary="How a visitor's request reaches the computer, step by step">

1. A visitor picks "Look something up" or "Check a price" in the Mana app and writes a question.
2. The app sends it to the computer's public address, a GurtOS Server on Vultr reached through GurtOS remote access.
3. The computer checks it. Only those two kinds are allowed (deny by default). Nothing about the computer, its owner, keys or files may be asked. At most 3 per guest and 10 from all guests per hour, and 3 waiting at once. A refusal comes back in plain words.
4. It becomes an ordinary GurtOS request on the owner's phone: "A guest asks for a price in the Mana app: ... Run it, with web tools and up to $0.25 of tool spending (the model is extra)?" The public status page never shows the guest's words.
5. After the owner approves, a task runs in an empty `guest` workspace with only the web tools, up to 25 cents of tool spending and no network of its own. A price check uses `apify.checkPrice` (1 cent). A request left unanswered for 30 minutes is dropped and nothing runs.
6. The app polls the computer and shows the answer and its cost.

The Fetch.ai agent sends strangers' requests through the same route, so both front doors share one set of limits that the computer enforces.

**Rules from Mana to Business.** Mana sends the rules to `POST /t/mana/policy` as GurtOS Business's own policy text. The computer parses them with Business's parser, refuses a line it cannot read, and turns the change into a request that lists the rules added and removed. Only an owner or an admin approving it applies it, and only if the question carries that exact text's hash. An approver cannot, and an agent cannot. In the merge gate, under Mana's version 1 a $7,000 rush order goes to the owner; version 2 changes one rule ("Over $5,000 goes to the Controller."); Rosa, an approver, tries to approve the change and is refused; the owner approves; the same $7,000 request now goes to Rosa.

**The status route.** `GET /t/mana/status` is public on purpose, so it holds little: task id, title, state, the latest step cut to 160 characters, cost, source and time; the question and amount of each waiting approval; counts by state; the total cost. It is read-only, and approving still needs the portal or a paired phone. It carries no prompts, tool calls, log lines or secrets.

</Details>
